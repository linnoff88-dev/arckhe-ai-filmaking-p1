Section 1 of 4·Principle

1.Effect isn’t realism

Start by clearing up the most costly confusion in this track: effect e realism aren’t the same thing. An effect is something that appears on screen—the explosion, the wave, the ship crashing. Realism is the feeling that it has 1weight, material, physical consequence. AI generates effects with astonishing ease; what it almost never delivers on its own is realism. And without realism, the effect becomes the opposite of what you wanted: instead of impressing, it gives itself away. That’s what we call “CGI overload” — that plastic sheen that makes your brain say “fake” before the scene even ends.

To understand what’s missing, it’s worth looking at how big-budget cinema still handles this—with techniques physical, even in the digital age. Not out of nostalgia, but for perceptual efficiency. These are six approaches that pursue what the lesson calls uncompromising physical realism, and each teaches something that applies directly to AI prompts.

Six ways to anchor in reality

A LED wall projects the environment behind the actor during filming, and the digital set’s real light reflects off the costume, creating correct shadows and contours—the light anchor the actor in the world. The practical fire brings the chaotic movement and organic smoke that overly clean digital fire lacks. The suspension by cables captures the body’s true inertia and the drag of fabric — physical weight is one of the hardest things to fake. A practical rain really wets clothing and pavement, and water physics is the fastest way to expose bad CGI. The thumbnails bring real dust and tactile texture; with the right framing, the brain reads them as structures at real scale. And the hybrid — physical foreground over digital background — use a detailed element in front to lend credibility to the generated background.

Stop and predict

Two shots show the same city on fire. In one, the fire is CGI: symmetrical flames licking the air in a smooth loop. In the other, it’s practical fire filmed and composited over the plate. Which seems more real —and what exactly does the brain detect?

See one possible answer

The second, almost always. The brain doesn’t measure “amount of fire”; it measures unpredictability. Real fire flickers chaotically, throws off irregular sparks, and distorts the air with heat. Clean digital fire is predictable — and predictability is the smell of something fake. Perfect movement looks artificial; chaos is what makes it feel real.

▸ Going deeper: why light is the first thing to get right optional

The happy path above is enough. This layer explains why the LED wall became standard—and can be skipped.

The reason the LED wall changed modern production isn’t the background image: it’s the light. When the digital environment emits real light on set, it falls on the face, the costume, the metal—and creates reflections, shadows, and edges that fit that world. Traditional compositing struggles precisely here: the light on the actor never matches the background perfectly, and the eye senses the seam. For AI, the lesson is direct: never leave the light source to chance. A prompt that defines where the light comes from, its color, and how hard it is already anchors the character in the space before any effect comes in. Light comes first—and the order of the cinematic design is light, texture, movement, atmosphere, emotion.

Section 2 of 4·Chaos

2.The chaos that convinces the brain

Bring together what the six techniques teach in a single sentence, and you have the secret to realism: perfect movement looks fake; chaos looks real. This goes against a beginner's instinct to ask the generator for “perfect flames,” “crystal-clear water,” and “smooth movement”—and get back a sterile image that looks like an old video game. The physical world isn't clean. Fire flickers unpredictably; water splashes at irregular angles; dust drifts without a path; fabric drags with a delay. For millions of years, the brain has used these imperfections to tell what's real from what's not.

For AI, this becomes a practical, almost paradoxical instruction: ask for the the right flaw. Instead of “perfect flames,” write “chaotic flames, organic sparks, irregular rising smoke.” Instead of “smooth water,” “unpredictable splashes, uneven flow.” You're not asking for mess — you're asking for physics of that material, described through the lens of real behavior. This is the lesson’s second prompting principle: always describe movement through the lens of physics.

The same principle outside disaster cinema

It applies beyond the dystopian world where these techniques shine — post-apocalyptic settings, atmospheric disasters, infrastructure in ruins, isolated survival.2 In a beverage ad, the uneven condensation on the can and the droplet running crooked are what make the product look cold and real; a surface that’s too clean looks like a cheap 3D render. In a music video, dust floating in the beam of light gives the air texture. The principle doesn’t change by genre: reality is marked by micro-imperfections, and you need to ask for them deliberately.

▸ Going deeper: chaos is no excuse for losing control optional

Optional layer—an important counterbalance so you don’t read “chaos” as “anything goes.”

“Chaos looks real” is a truth about texture of the movement — not about the scene structure. Smoke can rise unpredictably; the composition that contain it, no. Spatial geography, focal point, and light remain rigorously controlled. The reading mistake would be to generate a messy scene and call it “realistic.” The goal is the opposite: an impeccably organized frame, within which the materials behave with the irregularity of physics. Order in the architecture, chaos in the material — that’s where credibility comes from.

Section 3 of 4·Decision

3.VFX is a decision, not a button

Here’s the shift in mindset that gives this course its name. For an amateur, an effect is a button: there’s an explosion available, so use it; there’s slow-mo, so apply it; there’s smoke, so throw it in. For the director, every effect is a decision — and every decision has a cost. Every effect that comes in competes for the viewer’s attention with everything else in the frame. If it isn’t guiding the eye to what matters, it’s stealing attention than what matters. That’s why “too much CGI” gets tiring: it’s not too much quality, but a lack of hierarchy.

The question that turns an effect into direction is simple, and you should ask it every time: what does this effect do for the viewer? If the answer is “shows that I know how to do an effect,” cut it. If the answer is “creates a sense of danger,” “adds scale to the space,” “marks the decisive moment,” keep it — because now the effect serves perception.3 This is the thread that connects this lesson to all the others in the track: slow motion works when it says “this matters”; the particle works when it brings back atmosphere; action works when it creates clarity in chaos.

The workflow that puts emotion last

The lesson sums up cinematic realism as a fixed order: light → texture → movement → atmosphere → emotion. Notice that emotion comes last—not because it’s less important, but because it’s the result of the four previous layers working well. Beginners try to generate emotion directly (“an epic and moving scene”) and get nothing. The director builds light, then texture, then movement, then atmosphere — and emotion emerges. The effect lives in the middle of this chain, in the movement and atmosphere layers; it’s never the starting point or the destination.

Stop and predict

You generated a survival scene: ruins, fog, smoke, sparks, rain, lightning, debris flying — all at once. Technically flawless, and even so the viewer feels nothing. Looking at the idea of effect as decision: what probably went wrong?

See one possible answer

Every effect was turned on all at once. Without hierarchy, each element steals attention from the others, and none guides the eye—it’s CGI overload. The question “what this effect does for the viewer?” A strong scene usually has one dominant effect serving a focal point; everything else is subtle support. Scale without clarity is noise.

Section 4 of 4·Prompt

4.The block prompt and its limits

Decided what the effect works; what’s missing is the how ask. In professional AI workflows, the lesson recommends structuring the prompt in logical blocks — style

YAMLYAML is a text format that organizes information in pairs label: value, on separate lines. Here, it doesn’t need to be valid YAML: what matters is the discipline separating each decision (subject, light, physics, camera) into its own field, instead of dumping everything into one sentence.
— instead of one long, messy sentence. The reason is control: when each decision has its own field (subject, light, motion physics, camera path), you can change one without breaking the others, and the generator interprets each instruction more precisely.

Four rules govern these fields. Single function: one specific task per field. Physical focus: describe the movement through the lens of physics, with no vague adjectives. Define the light: never leave the light source to chance. Controlled camera: keep the camera path logical and anchored. The prompt below applies all four — and already incorporates the “organic chaos” from Section 2.

# Realistic VFX — prompt in blocks (YAML style) for a 5s shot # works in Seedance / Kling / Runway / Luma; one field, one decision subject: a survivor crossing a flooded, ruined street in a dead city. lighting: hard low sun from camera-left, warm key, deep cold shadows — never neutral. physics_motion: chaotic flames on a wreck, organic sparks, irregular smoke rising; water splashes unevenly underfoot. atmosphere: drifting haze, floating ash, heat distortion above the fire. texture: soaked fabric clinging, wet asphalt reflecting the key, chipped concrete. camera: slow grounded dolly-in, steady eye-level, single intentional move — no orbit stacking. style: cinematic, high contrast, photoreal, no plastic CGI sheen.

Where AI still breaks down

No prompt is a guarantee, because AI brings closer reality — it doesn’t really understand physics.4 Three failures are common, and you need to recognize them at a glance. The motion drift: objects that “float” or lose their trajectory, as if gravity had switched off. A time break: unnatural acceleration, glitches, jumps in the rhythm of movement. And the artificial texture: surfaces that become too smooth and plastic-looking, exactly the opposite of the chaos you asked for. In either case, the answer isn’t frustration: it’s iteration. Rework it, adjust a field, generate it again—iteration is a normal part of the creative process, not a sign of failure.

Capture this module as the track’s entry point: effect is not realism; realism is weight, light, and organic chaos; and every effect is a decision in service of perception. The assignment the instructor proposes is the best test—choose one of the six techniques and build a realistic five-second scene with a block-based prompt. Just one, made with care, teaches more than ten packed with effects. The next lessons take three of these effects — time, atmosphere, and action — and unpack them one by one.

Before turning the page, hold on to the question you’ll carry through the rest of the course—and that is the very definition of directing an effect instead of applying it: what does this effect do for the viewer? If you don’t have an answer, the effect is still just a button. The next lesson puts that question to the test with the most seductive and most misused effect of all: slow motion.
